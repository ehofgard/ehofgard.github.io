Learning Lattice Parameters from Powder X-Ray Diffraction Data Using Invariants

Machine learning models have been used to aid in predicting lattice parameters from powder X-ray diffraction (XRD) data. The lattice parameters of a crystal are subject to conventions (e.g., primitive vs. conventional cells) and can change discontinuously at symmetry boundaries between Bravais lattices. We present an invariant representation of the reciprocal lattice, based on spherical harmonic projections, that is independent of cell convention, differentiable, and invertible. When predicting unit cells from powder X-ray diffraction data, replacing lattice parameters with this representation as the learning target reduces errors by a up to a factor of five.

Introduction

Powder X-ray diffraction (XRD) is a core tool for characterizing materials. In general, traditional methods extract peak positions and then assess trial unit cells in order to determine the correct lattice parameters, a process known as indexing. This can be difficult in the presence of severe peak overlap, for low-symmetry crystals, or for materials with a large unit cell axis (dominant zones). Discriminating between competing solutions can become an art form relying on the prior knowledge of the crystallographer. There has been growing interest in using machine learning (ML) to narrow this search.

Recent work trains neural networks to predict the six lattice parameters \((a, b, c, \alpha, \beta, \gamma)\) directly from a powder pattern . However, these lattice parameters are not an ideal target for an ML model, as they depend on the choice of unit cell and can change discontinuously near symmetry boundaries. We instead predict the lattice bispectrum, a smooth descriptor that is invariant to the choice of cell, and recover the lattice parameters by inverting it. With the same architecture, this reduces length error on MP-20 from 11.1% to 2.4% and angle error from 12.2% to 2.5%.

Unit Cell Representations

A lattice can be generated by three vectors, whose lengths and relative angles give the lattice parameters. However, infinitely many sets of vectors generate the same lattice. Unit cell reduction procedures select a canonical set, but they are defined piecewise across crystal systems. Small continuous changes in a lattice can therefore produce discontinuous jumps in the lattice parameters near symmetry boundaries, even though the underlying geometry changes smoothly. This has practical consequences for ML, as the lattice parameters are a discontinuous learning target. A model predicting lattice parameters must implicitly learn a single consistent convention.

Interpolation between a cubic and a triclinic lattice. The bispectrum (top row) varies smoothly along this path, while the reduced-cell distance \(S^6\) (bottom left) is non-smooth because of changes in the chosen reduced basis.

In dominant zone structures, usually those with one axis much longer than the others, most reflections can be indexed with only two Miller indices. The diffraction pattern then leaves the remaining lattice geometry underconstrained, which is difficult for both traditional indexing algorithms and neural networks.

Lattice Bispectrum

A useful lattice descriptor should be independent of primitive cell choice, continuous under smooth lattice deformations, and expressed in reciprocal space, where diffraction operates. Our workflow predicts such a descriptor from the XRD pattern, inverts it to obtain a set of lattice vectors, and then converts these vectors to standard lattice parameters.

Workflow for predicting the bispectrum and inverting it to obtain lattice parameters.

Given reciprocal lattice vectors \(\{\mathbf{b}_1, \mathbf{b}_2, \mathbf{b}_3\}\), each reciprocal lattice point is \(\mathbf{G} = h\mathbf{b}_1 + k\mathbf{b}_2 + l\mathbf{b}_3\) with \(h, k, l \in \mathbb{Z}\). We take all points within a cutoff \(k_{\max}\), which corresponds to the limiting resolution of the detector, and write the lattice as

\[\rho(\mathbf{k}) = \sum_{|\mathbf{G}| \leq k_{\max}} \delta(\mathbf{k} - \mathbf{G})\]

We then expand \(\rho\) using spherical harmonics \(Y_{lm}\) for the angular part and radial basis functions \(g_n\) for the radial part, which gives the coefficients

\[c_{nlm} = \sum_{|\mathbf{G}| \leq k_{\max}} Y_{lm}(\hat{\mathbf{G}})\, g_n(|\mathbf{G}|)\]

Invariant descriptors can be obtained from successive tensor products of these coefficients. The simplest is the power spectrum, \(\sum_m \lvert c_{nlm} \rvert^2\), but this loses information about correlations between different \(l\) channels. To retain more information, we couple coefficients from three channels \((l_1, l_2, l_3)\) into scalars through a symmetrized Clebsch-Gordan tensor product, following the standard construction from the atomistic ML literature :

\[\mathbf{B} = \big(\mathbf{c}_{l_3} \otimes (\mathbf{c}_{l_1} \otimes \mathbf{c}_{l_2})\big)^{(0_e \oplus 0_o)}\]

where \((0_e \oplus 0_o)\) indicates that we keep scalars and pseudoscalars. All pseudoscalars vanish for Bravais lattices since they are centrosymmetric, but we retain these channels for generality. In this work, we use \(l_{\max} = 6\) and 10 Bessel radial basis functions, with \(k_{\max} \approx 2/3\) Å⁻¹. Each row of the resulting descriptor corresponds to a radial basis function and each column to an allowed coupling, so the bispectrum can be visualized as a 2D heatmap.

This construction has the following properties:

  • Independent of primitive cell choice: Any set of primitive vectors generates the same lattice points, and \(c_{nlm}\) is a sum over these points.
  • Invariant under \(O(3)\): The construction is rotationally invariant, and every reciprocal lattice is centrosymmetric. This is consistent with powder diffraction, which is insensitive to orientation and measures the same intensity at \(\mathbf{G}\) and \(-\mathbf{G}\).
  • Smooth: The bispectrum varies smoothly in \(L^2\) norm under continuous lattice deformations.
  • Reflects lattice symmetry: Only certain \((l, m)\) channels can be non-zero for a given point group, so higher-symmetry lattices produce more zero entries. For example, only \(l = 0, 4, 6, 8, \ldots\) contribute for a cubic lattice.
Sample bispectra for lattices with varying levels of symmetry. The sparsity pattern reflects the symmetry of the lattice, with higher-symmetry lattices producing more zero entries.

Inverting the Bispectrum

The bispectrum is not guaranteed to be a one-to-one mapping, but it can be inverted empirically . Since the computation of \(\mathbf{B}\) is differentiable in PyTorch, we can use L-BFGS-B to optimize a lattice until its bispectrum matches the target. To initialize the optimization, we retrieve the 15 nearest entries from a precomputed database of Materials Project bispectra and use these as starting points, stopping once a candidate reaches a sufficiently low bispectrum loss. The inversion yields a set of vectors spanning the lattice, which we convert to conventional lattice parameters using pymatgen or cctbx.

Predicting the Bispectrum from XRD

We use a transformer to predict the bispectrum from the XRD pattern. Previous works have used convolutional neural networks (CNNs), but these are translation invariant and cannot directly relate peaks across the full pattern. Self-attention allows every peak to attend to every other peak, and preserves absolute peak positions. To evaluate the effect of the representation, we train the same architecture to output either the lattice parameters or the bispectrum, changing only the output dimension. Both the true and predicted lattices are passed through cctbx before computing errors, so that all comparisons are made in a consistent setting.

Results

Simulated Patterns

We train on four sets of simulated patterns: MP-20 (Materials Project structures with up to 20 atoms per unit cell , 27K patterns), the full Materials Project (93K patterns), and augmented versions of each (270K and 930K patterns). The physics-based augmentations include strain, peak intensity and width variation, and background noise. Predicting the bispectrum reduces length, angle, and volume MAPE by a factor of 1.8 to 5 on every dataset.

Mean absolute percentage error (MAPE) for direct prediction and bispectrum + inversion using the same transformer. MP-20 models are evaluated on the MP-20 test set and MP-Full models on the MP-Full test set.

This improvement is likely due in part to the bispectrum preserving lattice symmetry. Without separate models for each crystal system, direct prediction may yield values that do not align with the correct Bravais lattice, such as cubic angles that are not exactly 90°. The bispectrum does not show this behavior, as it already encodes the symmetry of the lattice.

Dominant Zones

We define dominant zone patterns as those with at least 50% of their total intensity in a single zero-index family of reflections (\(h = 0\), \(k = 0\), or \(l = 0\)), which applies to 1,100 of the 30,000 structures in the full Materials Project test set. Using the model trained on the augmented full Materials Project, 76.6% of bispectrum predictions on these structures have both length and angle MAPE below 5%, compared to 5.8% of direct predictions. Direct prediction also often mispredicts symmetry-fixed angles of 90° and 120°, whereas bispectrum inversion largely preserves them.

Parity plots for dominant zone structures, for bispectrum inversion (left) and direct prediction (right). Top: predicted vs. true longest axis. Bottom: predicted vs. true lattice angles. The columns at 90° and 120° are angles fixed by symmetry.

Experimental Data

We first evaluate on the 134 experimental RRUFF patterns used to evaluate Crystalyze , a generative model that predicts full crystal structures. Although our model predicts only the lattice, the bispectrum approach achieves performance comparable to Crystalyze:

Method (trained on MP-20 Aug) Length MAE (Å) Length MAPE (%) Angle MAPE (%) Volume MAPE (%)
Bispectrum + inversion (ours) 0.91 14.4 8.9 35.7
Crystalyze 0.90 16.1 9.9 43.4
Direct prediction (ours) 1.86 30.9 10.3 94.8

On the 240 RRUFF patterns used to evaluate AlphaDiffract , our model underperforms AlphaDiffract, particularly for angles. This is expected given that AlphaDiffract trains on 31 million patterns, about 33 times the size of our training set. Our length MAE is nonetheless relatively close (2.50 vs. 2.11 Å). Scaling our model by 1.5x and 2x gave only small improvements, which suggests that model capacity is not the primary bottleneck.

We also evaluate on single-phase CNRS patterns from the opXRD database , where both models show larger errors than on RRUFF. With our original training setup, direct prediction outperforms the bispectrum model on these patterns (length MAPE of 39.2% vs. 43.4%, angle MAPE of 8.9% vs. 15.0%). Since our background noise augmentation was fit to RRUFF, we hypothesize that this is due to a mismatch in noise. Retraining with CNRS-informed noise reduces the bispectrum length MAPE to 26.6%, and using peak lists as input reduces it further to 17.3%. With CNRS-informed noise, 25% of bispectrum predictions have both length and angle MAPE below 5%, whereas fewer than 5% of direct predictions reach this on any experimental dataset. This suggests that noise augmentation fit to one experimental dataset may not generalize to others.

Limitations and Future Work

There are several limitations to our approach:

  • Inversion cost: Inversion takes a median of 88 seconds per structure on a single CPU core with 15 starting candidates. This is parallelizable, but much slower than a forward pass of the model.
  • Out-of-distribution patterns: For experimental patterns far from the training data, bispectrum predictions are further from the ground truth. This makes inversion more difficult and can degrade the predicted lattice parameters.
  • Simulation to real gap: Performance on experimental data remains below performance on simulated data. Closing this gap will likely require larger datasets and augmentations that better capture experimental artifacts.
  • Lack of a standard benchmark: Different works use different training data, splits, and augmentations. A shared benchmark with a fixed training set, augmentation protocol, and test split would allow direct comparison between methods, and would ideally include experimental data currently scattered across personal repositories.

The lattice bispectrum could also be useful for other crystallographic ML tasks. For example, structure generation models could condition on the bispectrum rather than on raw lattice parameters. Since determining a lattice from an XRD pattern is fundamentally a one-to-many problem, a generative version of our approach that samples candidate lattices would be a natural extension. Incorporating the structure factor could also yield a descriptor sensitive to both the lattice and the atomic basis, which could aid in full structure determination.

Conclusion

We introduce an invariant, invertible representation of the reciprocal lattice and show that using it as the prediction target improves lattice parameter prediction from powder XRD. While ML for science often focuses on inputs and architectures, these results suggest that the choice of target deserves more attention, particularly when the standard representation is ambiguous or discontinuous.
